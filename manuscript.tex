{\large \textbf{Beyond Accuracy: Investigating Confidence, Calibration,
and Fairness Gaps in a Small Machine Learning Classifier}}

\vspace{0.8em}
{\normalsize Shaurya Gupta}\\
{\small Independent Researcher \quad \href{mailto:shauryagupta042@gmail.com}{shauryagupta042@gmail.com}}

\vspace{0.5em}
{\small \textit{Keywords:} machine learning; calibration; fairness; AUC;
bootstrap uncertainty; small datasets; AI evaluation}
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
Machine learning classifiers are usually evaluated with a single number: the
area under the ROC curve, or AUC. It measures ranking quality---how well the
model separates positive cases from negative ones. What it doesn't measure is
whether the model's predicted probabilities are actually reliable, whether the
result would hold up under different data samples, or whether errors fall
equally on all groups.

I investigated these gaps by training three classifiers on a small
benchmark dataset (1,470 records) and evaluating them through three additional
lenses: calibration error, bootstrap uncertainty intervals, and demographic
fairness metrics. All three models achieved AUC scores above 0.77, which
looks acceptable. But calibration analysis showed systematic overconfidence,
with Expected Calibration Error between 4.1\% and 6.5\% before correction.
Bootstrap resampling showed that the 95\% confidence interval for AUC spanned
roughly 17 percentage points---meaning the reported score is sensitive to
which samples happened to end up in the test set. And age-based fairness
analysis found an Equalized Odds Difference of 0.266, a substantial disparity
that the AUC score completely obscures.

The main finding is that a high accuracy score can coexist with miscalibrated
probabilities, fragile results, and unequal errors---all at the same time.
None of these problems require exotic statistics to detect. They just require
asking the right questions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When someone says an AI system is ``82\% accurate,'' it sounds pretty solid.
More than four out of five predictions correct. But I kept running into a
question I couldn't shake: what does that number actually tell you?

The more I read about machine learning evaluation, the more I realized that
accuracy---and even the more sophisticated version called AUC---leaves out some
things that matter. It doesn't tell you whether the model's confidence
estimates are trustworthy. It doesn't tell you how much the score might vary
if you tested on a different batch of data. It doesn't tell you whether the
errors fall equally on everyone, or whether some groups get worse predictions
than others.

These gaps might not matter much for a model classifying photos of cats versus
dogs. But they matter a lot when the predictions affect real decisions about
people---hiring, lending, medical triage, academic evaluation. In those
contexts, someone is actually acting on the model's output. If the confidence
numbers are off, or if the results are fragile, or if certain groups get
systematically worse predictions, a good AUC score doesn't tell you any of
that.

I wanted to test this directly. I trained three classifiers on a small
organizational dataset and evaluated them beyond AUC: with calibration
analysis, bootstrap confidence intervals, and fairness metrics across
demographic groups. The goal wasn't to find the best-performing model.
It was to see how much the standard accuracy metric conceals.

The results were more interesting than I expected. Every extra evaluation
lens I applied revealed something the AUC score had been hiding.

\section{Background}

\subsection{What AUC Measures}

The dominant metric for evaluating binary classifiers (models that output
one of two categories) is AUC, the area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve. For a classifier predicting whether something is
positive or negative, AUC measures discrimination: if you randomly pick
one true positive and one true negative, how often does the model assign
the higher score to the positive case? An AUC of 0.80 means it gets this
right 80\% of the time.

That's genuinely useful. But AUC says nothing about the actual probability
values the model outputs. Two models can have identical AUC scores while one
gives well-calibrated probabilities and the other gives values that are
systematically too high or too low. The ranking is the same; the numbers
attached to each rank can be completely different.

\subsection{Calibration: When Probabilities Should Mean Something}

A well-calibrated model is one where the predicted probability actually
matches the observed frequency. If it says a case has 70\% probability of
the positive outcome, then roughly 70\% of cases with that predicted
probability should actually turn out positive. The weather forecast analogy
is probably the clearest: a meteorologist who says ``70\% chance of rain''
is calibrated if it rains about 70\% of the time when they make that
prediction---not 30\% of the time and not 95\% of the time.

Guo et al.\ introduced Expected Calibration Error (ECE) as a practical
metric for this \cite{guo2017}. It divides predictions into bins by
confidence level and measures the average gap between predicted probability
and actual outcome rate across bins. A perfectly calibrated model has
ECE~=~0. Random forests and boosted trees tend to be overconfident---they
push probabilities toward the extremes more than the data justifies
\cite{niculescu2005}. This is worth checking because if you're using
confidence scores to prioritize which predictions to act on, overconfident
numbers give you the wrong impression of how certain the model really is.

\subsection{Bootstrap Uncertainty: Is the Score Stable?}

Any test-set evaluation is one particular draw from a noisy process.
With a small dataset, the specific examples that end up in the test set
can move the AUC score by several percentage points. Bootstrap resampling
quantifies this: you repeatedly resample the data (with replacement), retrain
or re-evaluate, and measure the spread of outcomes. The resulting confidence
interval says something honest---this is the range the reported metric could
have plausibly taken, not just a single lucky or unlucky number.

\subsection{Fairness: Whose Errors?}

Even a classifier with good average accuracy can make worse predictions for
some demographic groups than others. Hardt et al.\ formalized two useful
metrics for measuring this \cite{hardt2016}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Demographic Parity Difference (DPD)}: the gap in positive
    prediction rates between two groups.
  \item \textbf{Equalized Odds Difference (EOD)}: the gap in true positive
    \emph{and} false positive rates between groups. A model satisfies
    equalized odds when its error structure is the same regardless of group
    membership.
\end{itemize}

A model can score perfectly on both DPD and EOD and still produce unjust
outcomes if the label itself encodes historical bias---but measuring these
metrics is at least a starting point for understanding whether the classifier
treats groups differently.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

I used the IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset, a synthetic
organizational benchmark publicly available through Kaggle
\cite{ibm2015}. It contains 1,470 employee records described by 35
variables: job characteristics, satisfaction ratings, demographics, and
compensation. The binary outcome is whether the employee left the
organization (attrition); 241 of 1,470 records (16.1\%) are labeled
as attrition events.

The dataset is synthetic---generated by IBM data scientists for
educational purposes, not derived from any real workforce. This matters
for interpreting results: the findings describe model behavior on a
simulated benchmark, not on real employment decisions. That's a meaningful
limitation. It also means the data can be used and shared without privacy
issues, which is why I chose it.

I split the data 80/20 (training: 1,176 records, test: 294) using stratified
sampling to preserve the 16.1\% attrition rate in both sets.

\subsection{Classifiers}

I trained three classifiers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR)}: a simple linear model that
    produces inherently calibrated probabilities under well-specified
    conditions. Useful as a calibration baseline.
  \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)}: an ensemble of decision trees. Widely
    used in practice on tabular data \cite{breiman2001}.
  \item \textbf{CatBoost (CB)}: a gradient boosting algorithm with native
    categorical feature handling \cite{prokhorenkova2018}.
\end{enumerate}

I didn't tune hyperparameters. The goal was to see how these classifiers
look under additional evaluation metrics at representative performance
levels, not to squeeze out the best possible AUC.

\subsection{Evaluation}

I evaluated four things:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AUC} on the held-out test set (the standard metric).
  \item \textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)} before and after
    post-hoc calibration correction using isotonic regression.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence intervals} on AUC: 1,000 resamples,
    reporting the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
  \item \textbf{Fairness metrics} (DPD and EOD) for gender (Male/Female)
    and age group (under~40 / over~40).
\end{enumerate}

All code is publicly available at:
\url{https://github.com/SilverCoin256/accuracy-isnt-enough}

\section{Results}

\subsection{AUC: The Baseline Picture}

All three classifiers achieved AUC above 0.77 on the test set (Table~\ref{tab:auc}).
CatBoost performed best at 0.818. These scores are well above the 0.5 random
baseline. If this were the only evaluation, the conclusion would be that all
three models work reasonably well, with CatBoost the winner.

\label{tab:auc}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.777 \\
Random Forest       & 0.802 \\
CatBoost            & 0.818 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Calibration: The Confidence Scores Were Off}

Table~\ref{tab:ece} shows Expected Calibration Error before and after
applying isotonic regression as a post-hoc calibration correction.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Expected Calibration Error (ECE) before and after isotonic regression calibration correction.}
\label{tab:ece}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{ECE (before)} & \textbf{ECE (after)} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.041 & 0.028 \\
Random Forest       & 0.065 & 0.032 \\
CatBoost            & 0.053 & 0.031 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Random Forest had the highest pre-calibration ECE at 6.5\%. CatBoost
was at 5.3\%. These numbers mean, on average, the models' predicted
probabilities were off from actual outcome rates by about 5--6 percentage
points. Post-hoc calibration reduced ECE substantially for all three
models, which confirms the miscalibration was a real pattern rather than
noise. The fact that a simple correction layer works suggests the problem
is structural---these models systematically over-report confidence in
their predictions.

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} shows a reliability diagram for CatBoost
before calibration. The diagonal represents perfect calibration; the
model's curve deviates above the diagonal in the high-confidence region,
where predictions are more confident than the actual outcome rates warrant.
That high end is the most concerning: cases where the model is very
confident are exactly the ones a decision-maker is most likely to act on
without additional review.

  The dashed diagonal represents perfect calibration. The model is
  overconfident, particularly in the high-confidence region (right side

\subsection{Bootstrap Results: How Stable Are These Scores?}

Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} shows 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals
on AUC for each model.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals on AUC (1,000 resamples).}
\label{tab:bootstrap}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{Width} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.777 & [0.704, 0.845] & 0.141 \\
Random Forest       & 0.802 & [0.731, 0.869] & 0.138 \\
CatBoost            & 0.818 & [0.748, 0.888] & 0.140 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

These intervals are wide. A 95\% interval spanning about 14 percentage
points for CatBoost (0.748 to 0.888) means that if the training and test
split had turned out slightly differently, the reported AUC could plausibly
have been anywhere in that range. The ``winning'' AUC of 0.818 is a point
estimate from one particular draw of 294 test examples out of 1,470 total.
It's not a stable measurement in the way that, say, a physical constant is.

I found this one of the more surprising results. The point estimate looks
clean and decisive. The interval around it says the underlying result is
genuinely uncertain---not because the analysis was done wrong, but because
1,470 data points genuinely don't support more precision than this. On
a larger dataset the same model might produce a much narrower interval.
On this one, it doesn't.

Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap} shows the bootstrap distribution of CatBoost AUC
scores across all 1,000 resamples.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/fig2_bootstrap_distribution.png}
\caption{Bootstrap distribution of CatBoost AUC across 1,000 resamples.
  The vertical dashed line marks the observed test AUC. The shaded region
  shows the 95\% confidence interval. The distribution is fairly spread,
  showing meaningful sensitivity to which samples ended up in each set.}
\label{fig:bootstrap}

\subsection{Fairness: Age-Based Disparities Were Large}

Table~\ref{tab:fairness} shows demographic parity difference and equalized
odds difference for gender (Male vs.\ Female) and age group (under~40
vs.\ over~40), using CatBoost predictions.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Fairness metrics for CatBoost. DPD = Demographic Parity Difference;
  EOD = Equalized Odds Difference. Smaller values are better.}
\label{tab:fairness}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Group} & \textbf{DPD} & \textbf{EOD} \\
\midrule
Gender (Male vs.\ Female) & 0.015 & 0.121 \\
Age (Under~40 vs.\ Over~40) & 0.031 & 0.266 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Gender disparity was small: DPD of 0.015 and EOD of 0.121. The model
predicts attrition for male and female employees at similar rates and
with similar error structures. The age disparity was different. EOD of
0.266 means there was a 26-percentage-point gap in equalized odds between
younger and older employee groups---a substantial difference in how
accurate the model was for each group, despite having the same overall AUC.

To be clear: this finding has to be interpreted carefully on a synthetic
dataset. The IBM HR data was generated by IBM, not collected from a real
organization, so the age disparity might reflect an artifact of how the
simulation was constructed rather than a pattern that generalizes. But
that's exactly the point: the age-based EOD of 0.266 showed up regardless,
and it's the kind of thing the overall AUC score of 0.818 never hints at.

Figure~\ref{fig:fairness} shows the EOD comparison across groups.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What These Results Say Together}

Taken together, these findings describe a model that looks good on the
standard metric but has three distinct problems underneath it. The
calibration issue means that acting on the model's confidence scores at
face value would lead to systematically overconfident decisions---the model
thinks it's more sure than it is. The bootstrap issue means that even the
AUC number itself is less stable than it looks---on a dataset this size,
reported accuracy is sensitive to data splits. And the fairness issue
means that the model's errors aren't distributed equally, at least across
age groups.

None of these problems are catastrophic. The model isn't useless. But
they're real, and none of them show up in the single AUC number that
most evaluations stop at.

\subsection{Why Small Datasets Make This Worse}

All three problems are worse on small datasets than on large ones. A
larger training set would give the model more signal, reducing calibration
error. A larger test set would narrow the bootstrap confidence intervals.
More examples per demographic group would give more precise fairness
estimates.

The IBM HR dataset has 1,470 records. That's small by the standards of
modern ML, but it's not unusual for the kinds of settings where AI systems
are actually being built---school districts, small companies, local
healthcare organizations. These datasets are private, hard to obtain,
and often not much larger than this one. The evaluation problems I found
here aren't unusual edge cases; they're likely common.

\subsection{Post-Hoc Calibration Helps}

One straightforward fix is worth noting: post-hoc calibration (fitting a
correction function on a held-out calibration set) reduced ECE by roughly
40--50\% for all three models. That's encouraging. It suggests the
miscalibration isn't baked into the model's fundamental behavior---it can
be corrected with an extra step. The cost is setting aside part of the
data for calibration fitting, which is a tradeoff on a small dataset.

\subsection{What I'd Do Differently}

A few things I'd want to explore with more resources: first, running the
same experiment across multiple real-world datasets to check whether these
patterns hold generally or are specific to this benchmark. Second, looking
at zone-level calibration---whether miscalibration concentrates in
uncertain or confident predictions, which matters differently for
decision-making. Third, checking whether the age disparity persists after
calibration correction, or whether it's partly an artifact of miscalibration.

\section{Conclusion}

I started this project because the phrase ``82\% accurate'' seemed both
reassuring and underspecified. After running calibration analysis,
bootstrap uncertainty estimation, and fairness metrics on three classifiers,
I'm more convinced that single-number accuracy summaries consistently
hide important information.

The additional evaluations I ran aren't technically exotic. Calibration
error, bootstrap intervals, and fairness metrics are all implementable in
Python with standard libraries, using the same data that would go into
computing AUC. The barrier isn't skill or data access. It's just the
habit of asking: what else might be wrong that this one number doesn't show?

All classifiers passed a basic AUC threshold while failing, to varying
degrees, on calibration, stability, and fairness. That doesn't make them
useless. It makes them more complicated than their headline score suggests.
And in settings where AI predictions shape real decisions, that complexity
is worth knowing about.
