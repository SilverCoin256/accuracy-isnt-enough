{\large \textbf{Beyond Accuracy: Investigating Calibration, Bootstrap
Uncertainty, and Fairness Gaps in Machine Learning Classifiers}}

\vspace{0.8em}
{\normalsize Shaurya Gupta}\\
{\small Independent Researcher, shauryagupta042@gmail.com}

\vspace{0.5em}
{\small \textit{Keywords:} machine learning; calibration; fairness; AUC;
bootstrap uncertainty; small datasets; classifier evaluation}
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
Most people evaluating a machine learning model stop at one number: AUC,
the area under the ROC curve. If it clears some threshold---say, 0.75 or
0.80---the model gets called ``good enough'' and the evaluation ends there.
I wanted to find out what that habit actually hides.

I trained three classifiers---Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and
CatBoost---on the IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset (1,470
employee records) and then ran three checks that AUC never runs for you.
First, I measured calibration: do the model's probability outputs actually
match real outcome rates, or do they just rank things in the right order
while being numerically wrong? I used Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
before and after applying an isotonic regression correction. Second, I built
bootstrap confidence intervals on AUC using 1,000 resamples of the test
set---because any single 294-record test split is just one of many possible
draws, and I wanted to see how much the score would bounce around. Third, I
computed fairness metrics (Demographic Parity Difference and Equalized Odds
Difference) for gender and age groups to check whether prediction errors
fell equally on different groups of people.

The results were more uncomfortable than I expected. All three models
cleared 0.75 AUC. CatBoost's probability outputs were off by 8.6 percentage
points on average before correction. Every model's bootstrap confidence
interval was about 15--16 percentage points wide, meaning a ``0.762 AUC''
could just as plausibly have been 0.68 or 0.84 on a different random split.
The age-group fairness gap reached 0.258 for CatBoost---a 26-point
difference in how errors were distributed between younger and older
employees---while the AUC score showed nothing wrong at all. The point of
this paper is not that these problems are unusual. It's that they showed up
together, in the same ordinary models, without any signal from the metric
everyone relies on.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When someone calls an AI system ``82\% accurate,'' it sounds reassuring. More
than four out of five predictions right. But I kept running into a question
I couldn't shake while working on this project: what does that number actually
tell you, and what does it leave out?

The standard evaluation metric for binary classifiers is AUC---area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve. It measures whether the model ranks
positive cases above negative ones, and an AUC of 0.80 means it gets that
ordering right 80\% of the time.\textsuperscript{1} That's genuinely useful.
But it took me a while to realize how much it doesn't answer. It says nothing
about whether the model's probability outputs are numerically trustworthy---a
model can rank cases perfectly while being wildly wrong about the actual
probability numbers it outputs. It says nothing about whether the score you
got would hold up on a different random split of the same data. And it says
nothing about whether the model's errors are landing equally on all groups
of people in the dataset, or piling up on some groups more than others.

None of that might matter much for a classifier sorting spam emails. But if
a model is being used to make predictions about hiring, healthcare, or
anything that affects real people, those are exactly the questions someone
should be asking---and AUC doesn't raise them.

The first gap is calibration. When a model says ``70\% probability,'' that
number should actually mean something---roughly 70 out of 100 people with
that score should have the outcome.\textsuperscript{2} Expected Calibration
Error (ECE) measures how far off those probability outputs are from the real
rates.\textsuperscript{3} I'd read that tree-based and boosting models tend
to get overconfident---pushing their predictions toward certainty more than
the data actually supports---so I wanted to see how bad it was in
practice.\textsuperscript{4}

The second gap is stability. My test set had 294 records. That's just one
random partition of 1,470 total. Bootstrap resampling lets you repeatedly
redraw the test set with replacement and re-check the score each time,
giving you a confidence interval rather than a single number. I expected
the intervals to be wide; I wanted to see how wide.

The third gap is fairness. A model can have fine average accuracy while
making much worse errors for specific subgroups. Hardt et al.\ developed
two metrics for measuring this: Demographic Parity Difference (DPD), which
compares positive prediction rates between groups, and Equalized Odds
Difference (EOD), which compares the error structure between
groups.\textsuperscript{5} I chose gender and age as the groups to check
because they're present in the dataset and demographically meaningful in
an employment context. There's quite a bit of documented evidence that
trained models produce unequal error rates across demographic groups even
when headline performance looks fine.\textsuperscript{6}

My goal wasn't to build the best model. It was to take three ordinary
classifiers, check each of these gaps, and see whether AUC alone would
have caught anything.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

I used the IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset, which IBM created
synthetically for educational purposes and released through
Kaggle.\textsuperscript{7} It has 1,470 employee records with 35 variables
covering job role, satisfaction scores, demographics, and pay. The outcome
I was predicting was whether an employee left the company---241 out of 1,470
records, about 16\%, are labeled as attrition.

Because the data is synthetic, any patterns I found---especially in fairness
metrics---might reflect how IBM designed the simulation rather than anything
real. I knew that going in. I picked this dataset anyway because it's free,
widely used, and doesn't involve real people's private information.

I split the data 80/20 into a training set (1,176 records) and test set (294
records), stratified so the 16\% attrition rate appeared in both halves.

\subsection{Classifiers}

I trained three classifiers, all at default settings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR)}: a linear model. It's well-known
    for producing reasonable probability estimates, so I used it as a
    calibration baseline to compare against the other two.
  \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)}: a bagged ensemble of decision trees,
    which I included because it's one of the most common classifiers for
    tabular data.\textsuperscript{8}
  \item \textbf{CatBoost (CB)}: a gradient boosting method that handles
    categorical variables without manual encoding.\textsuperscript{9}
\end{enumerate}

I didn't tune any hyperparameters. The point wasn't to squeeze out the
best AUC---it was to see what the evaluation looked like on models that
perform at a typical level.

\subsection{What I Measured}

For each model I computed four things:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AUC} on the test set.
  \item \textbf{ECE} before and after applying an isotonic regression
    calibration correction. I held out 20\% of the training set (about 235
    records) to fit the correction layer, so the test set stayed untouched.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence intervals on AUC}: I resampled the
    294-record test set with replacement 1,000 times and took the 2.5th
    and 97.5th percentiles as the 95\% interval.
  \item \textbf{Fairness metrics} (DPD and EOD) comparing male vs.\ female
    and under-40 vs.\ over-40 employees, using CatBoost predictions at a
    0.5 threshold.
\end{enumerate}

All code is at: https://github.com/SilverCoin256/accuracy-isnt-enough

\section{Results}

\subsection{AUC}

All three models scored between 0.76 and 0.80 (Table~\ref{tab:auc}).
Logistic Regression came in highest at 0.798, which surprised me a bit---I
expected one of the ensembles to win. If I had stopped here, everything
would have looked fine.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Test-set AUC for each classifier.}
\label{tab:auc}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.798 \\
Random Forest       & 0.759 \\
CatBoost            & 0.762 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Calibration}

The calibration picture was messier (Table~\ref{tab:ece}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Expected Calibration Error before and after isotonic regression
correction.}
\label{tab:ece}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{ECE (before)} & \textbf{ECE (after)} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.047 & 0.058 \\
Random Forest       & 0.032 & 0.036 \\
CatBoost            & 0.086 & 0.066 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

CatBoost's pre-correction ECE was 8.6\%---its probability numbers were off
from real rates by about 9 percentage points on average. Looking at the
reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}), you can see the
overconfidence pretty clearly: the predictions bunch up above the diagonal
at the high end, meaning the model was more sure of itself than the outcomes
actually justified.

What I didn't expect: Logistic Regression's ECE went slightly \textit{worse}
after correction, from 0.047 to 0.058. LR is supposed to produce decent
probabilities naturally, so I think the isotonic regression correction just
added noise on top of something that was already okay---the calibration
fitting set only had around 235 records, which probably wasn't enough to
help a model that didn't really need fixing. CatBoost improved the most,
dropping from 0.086 to 0.066 after correction. That's a meaningful
improvement, and it's consistent with what I'd read about boosting models
tending to get overconfident.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{figures/fig1_reliability_diagram.png}
\caption{Reliability diagram for CatBoost before calibration correction.
  The dashed line shows perfect calibration. CatBoost's predicted
  probabilities cluster above the line at high confidence levels---it's
  more certain than it should be.}
\label{fig:calibration}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}

Every model's interval came out about 15--16 percentage points wide
(Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}). CatBoost's went from 0.676 to 0.838, which
means the exact same model could have reported anything in that range
depending on which 294 records happened to land in the test set.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals on AUC (1,000 resamples).}
\label{tab:bootstrap}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{Width} \\
\midrule
Logistic Regression & 0.798 & [0.716, 0.874] & 0.158 \\
Random Forest       & 0.759 & [0.686, 0.832] & 0.146 \\
CatBoost            & 0.762 & [0.676, 0.838] & 0.162 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

This bothered me more than the calibration numbers. At least miscalibration
has a correction you can apply. Wide confidence intervals don't have a fix---
they're just what 1,470 records can tell you, and reporting a single
point estimate without showing this is technically correct but pretty
misleading. Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap} shows how spread out the bootstrap
distribution actually gets.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/fig2_bootstrap_distribution.png}
\caption{Bootstrap distribution of CatBoost AUC across 1,000 resamples.
  The dashed vertical line is the observed test-set AUC; the dotted red
  lines are the 95\% interval boundaries. The distribution is wide enough
  that the reported score could easily have been 0.68 or 0.84 on a
  different random split.}
\label{fig:bootstrap}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Fairness Metrics}

Gender disparity was small---DPD of 0.018, EOD of 0.026 (Table~\ref{tab:fairness}).
The age gap was a different story. The EOD between under-40 and over-40
employees was 0.258, a 26-point difference in how errors were distributed
between the two groups. That's large. Meanwhile, CatBoost's aggregate AUC
was 0.762 and nothing in that number pointed at this at all.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Fairness metrics for CatBoost. DPD = Demographic Parity
  Difference; EOD = Equalized Odds Difference. Lower is better.}
\label{tab:fairness}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Group} & \textbf{DPD} & \textbf{EOD} \\
\midrule
Gender (Male vs.\ Female) & 0.018 & 0.026 \\
Age (Under 40 vs.\ Over 40)   & 0.062 & 0.258 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

I want to be careful about interpreting this. The data is synthetic, so
the gap could be a quirk of how IBM built the simulation. But I think the
real point is that the number showed up at all---and that checking for it
took maybe 20 extra lines of code beyond the AUC calculation.
Figure~\ref{fig:fairness} shows the comparison visually.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/fig3_fairness_comparison.png}
\caption{DPD and EOD by demographic group for CatBoost. The dashed line
  at 0.10 is a rough rule of thumb for acceptable disparity. The age-group
  EOD of 0.258 is more than double that.}
\label{fig:fairness}
\end{figure}

\section{Discussion}

The thing that stayed with me after running all of this is how the three
problems appeared together, in the same models, while the AUC numbers looked
fine the whole time. I expected some calibration error from CatBoost---I'd
read about that before starting. I expected the bootstrap intervals to be
wide, since 294 test records isn't much. What I didn't expect was the age
gap being 26 points. Finding all three in the same run, on a totally ordinary
benchmark with default hyperparameters, made the AUC numbers feel less like
a summary and more like a distraction.

Part of why the intervals were so wide is just the dataset size. With 1,470
total records and a test set of 294, any single random split is going to be
noisy. What I kept thinking about is that 1,470 records isn't actually that
small for a lot of real-world deployments. A school district, a regional
clinic, a small municipal program---those organizations are adopting
machine learning tools with datasets in exactly this range, and if they
only look at AUC, the intervals problem is invisible to them.

The calibration correction results taught me something I didn't expect.
CatBoost improved noticeably (ECE from 0.086 to 0.066), which made sense.
But correction made Logistic Regression slightly worse. The correction needs
data to fit on, and if the model is already decent, you're just adding
fitting noise from a small calibration set. It's not a free lunch---you
have to think about whether the model actually needs it.

The biggest limitation in this paper is that the dataset isn't real. IBM
generated it synthetically, which makes it great for reproducibility but
means the 26-point age gap might be entirely a product of how the simulation
was designed. I don't know whether a real workforce dataset would show the
same thing. That's the honest answer.

The question I'm actually most curious about from this work: does the age
fairness gap shrink after calibration correction? CatBoost had both the
worst calibration and the largest age EOD. I have a hunch those might be
connected---if the overconfidence is worse for one age group than the other,
fixing the probabilities might also reduce the fairness gap. I didn't
follow that thread here because the calibration correction didn't produce
clean results across all models, but it's where I'd start next.

\section{Conclusion}

I started this project with a pretty simple question: is AUC actually
telling you what you think it is? After working through the calibration,
bootstrap, and fairness checks, the honest answer is that it's telling you
one specific thing---rank ordering---and staying completely silent about
everything else.

ECE, bootstrap intervals, DPD, and EOD are all in scikit-learn. None of
them took more than an afternoon to add. What I got back for that was a much
more uncomfortable picture: a model that ranked cases well but had
overconfident probabilities, a reported AUC that could plausibly have been
0.68 or 0.84, and a 26-point error gap between age groups that nothing in
the AUC score would have told me to look for.

Whether those problems matter in a given situation depends on what the model
is being used for. But I think the more honest way to frame it is: you won't
know they're there unless you check. The three models in this paper all
passed the AUC bar.\textsuperscript{10} They didn't pass the other ones.
That gap between ``passes AUC'' and ``ready to use'' is what I was trying
to make visible here.
